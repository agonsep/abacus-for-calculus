A visual, inquiry-based activity in which students build the change-curve of y = x² and discover that a bending curve can have a perfectly straight pattern of change. Written for teachers; no formulas, rates, or calculations required.

## Short Introduction

Every curve carries a second curve inside it: the record of how much it changes from one value to the next. In this activity students build that second curve, the change-curve, first for a straight line and then for the parabola, and discover that a bending curve can have a perfectly straight pattern of change. Key terms for the activity: size-curve, change-curve, and level change-curve (a change-curve of constant height; the word flat is reserved for a curve such as y = 2 that does not climb at all).

## Learning Objectives

Students will construct a change-curve from y = x², observe that it rises in equal steps to form a straight line, and describe in their own words how the change in a function can have a pattern of its own across an interval. The aim is a durable visual image, the kind students can return to later when they meet rates of change and derivatives, rather than any calculation.

## Teacher Overview

This is the first of the Calculus Abacus's two leading uses: recognizing and interpreting patterns of change across an interval. It is deliberately distinct from the second use, which estimates how fast a function is changing at a single point. Here there are no rates to compute and no single point to settle on; the work is to notice how change behaves along a whole stretch of the curve.

We use y = x² because its change-curve reveals an especially clear pattern, a curved function whose differences form a straight, steadily rising line. The activity now begins with the straight line y = 2x, whose change-curve is level, so that students meet the simplest case first and the contrast with the parabola is vivid. Among simple choices, y = x² is the strongest for a first encounter because the pattern it produces is both striking and easy to read, and y = x³ and y = sin(x) extend the investigation for classes with time.

On the web application, each curve takes roughly 10 to 20 minutes, so a single curve pairs comfortably with Exercise 2 in one class period, while the full set of curves fills a period on its own. A physical Calculus Abacus works equally well. Expect students to come away noticing that the change-curve for a parabola is a rising straight line, and able to describe how the two curves are related in their own words.

## Student Activity

Set up the Calculus Abacus to show a good run of each curve at once. On the web application, a helpful starting configuration is midpoint 5, increment 1, max stones 50. Work through the steps below, taking time to look before moving on.

**START WITH A LINE:** Set the equation to y = 2x and fill the board. Build its change-curve: on the web application, press Find Differences (or place the change stones yourself with the + buttons); on a physical board, form the change between each pair of neighboring columns and set out orange stones for it. Move the change stones up so they sit above the size stones, aligned so their tops can be read as a curve. Evaluate what you see: the size-curve climbs steadily, and the change-curve is level, the same count in every column.

**PREDICT:** Now set the equation to y = x². Before building anything, study the original curve as it climbs across the interval, rising more steeply as it goes. Imagine building a second curve out of the change between each value and the next. Would that change-curve be level, rising, falling, or curved? Sketch your guess and write one sentence explaining it, keeping the change-curve of y = 2x in mind as your baseline.

**CONSTRUCT:** Using the Calculus Abacus, build the change-curve for y = x² the same way: press Find Differences (or place the stones by hand), then move the change stones up and align them above the size stones, so the change-curve takes shape beside the original across the whole stretch.

**OBSERVE:** Look only at the change-curve for a moment, and read it two ways. First from the stones: how does it move from left to right? Is it level, climbing, or falling? Does it change by the same amount at each step, or by different amounts? Then from the left panel: run your eye down the Change-Size column and describe what the numbers are doing.

**COMPARE:** Now hold both curves in view together. Where the original climbs gently, what is the change-curve doing? Where the original climbs steeply, how many change-size stones stand in the columns? Describe how the two are linked.

**REFLECT:** In your own words, describe the relationship between the original curve and its change-curve. Then finish this thought: the original was curved and kept getting steeper, but the change-curve turned out to be something else. What was it, and what does that tell you about how the change in y = x² behaves across the interval? Before you move on, fix the pairing in your mind, a bending curve beside a straight, steadily rising change-curve. It is a picture worth keeping.

**EXPLORE FURTHER:** If time allows, repeat the investigation for y = x³ with midpoint 0 and increment 0.5, and for y = sin(x) with the same settings. For each, describe the change-curve's shape in a sentence before checking the Change-Size column. On the sine board, notice what happens to the change stones where the curve stops rising.

## Reflection Questions

1. The original curve bends upward and keeps getting steeper, yet its change-curve came out straight. How can a curved function have a change that follows a straight line?

2. A straight line such as y = 2x has a level change-curve, while the parabola y = x² has a change-curve that rises steadily. What does the change-curve seem to capture about the original that its overall shape alone does not?

3. Picture extending the interval further to the right. What would you expect the change-curve to keep doing, and why?

4. Without using any numbers, how would you describe to a classmate the difference between a function that changes at a steady pace and one whose change keeps growing?

5. Our curved function produced a straight change-curve. What kind of original curve would produce a level change-curve, and what would that level line be telling us? Reason backward, from the shape of a change-curve to the behavior of the original.

## Expected Student Discoveries

* The change-curve climbs steadily, rising in equal steps from left to right.

* Although the original curve is curved, the change-curve looks like a straight line.

* Where the original curve climbs more steeply, from one column to the next, there are more change-size stones measuring the difference between the stones in neighboring columns.

* Some students notice that the change steps up by the same amount each time. With an increment of 1, the changes in value are the odd numbers, a regular and memorable pattern, though whether the stone counts show it literally depends on the scale and the fractional-stones setting; the robust observation is the steady climb.

* Students can learn the properties of the change-curve two ways: by aligning the change stones and observing the shape, or by looking at the Change-Size column in the left panel and observing the steady increase in the numbers.

* A productive leap: the change-curve is describing how quickly the original is rising, even though no one has used that language yet.

* Students begin to say things like "the change is itself changing, but in a regular way," which is exactly the insight the activity is built to surface.

## Common Misconceptions

* **Expecting the change-curve to copy the original.** Because the original is curved, students often assume its change-curve will curve too. The surprise that it comes out straight is the productive moment; the change-curve records how much the function moves from one neighbor to the next, not the function's own shape.

* **Concluding that a straight change-curve means the original is a straight line.** This is the mirror image of the first confusion, and the two are best addressed together. They are two different curves; here a straight change-curve comes from a decidedly curved original.

* **Treating "rising" and "rising faster" as the same thing.** A line rises, but its change-curve is level, because it climbs by the same amount at every step. The parabola's change-curve rises because it climbs by a little more each step. Separating these two ideas is the heart of the exercise.

* **Reading the change-curve as a list of numbers rather than a curve with a shape.** Encourage students to step back and take in its overall form.

## Teacher Notes

**Educational purpose.** This is students' first structured encounter with the idea that a function's change has a pattern of its own, and they meet it by building and seeing rather than by formula. The predict, construct, observe, compare, and reflect sequence is designed so the central result arrives as something students discover instead of something they are told.

**Conceptual significance.** By constructing the change-curve for y = x², students find that a curved function can have a perfectly regular, straight-line change. That single image, a bending curve paired with a steadily rising straight change-curve, is one students can hold onto. It is the conceptual seed of a good deal of what follows, but it is reached here as a picture, not a rule, and that is the point.

**Vocabulary.** A moment on terms at the start pays off: size-curve and change-curve for the two objects, and level for a change-curve of constant height. Note that y = 2x is straight but not flat, and its change-curve is level; only a constant curve such as y = 2 is flat.

**Why it is valuable.** Patterns of change are usually described in words or symbols long before students have anything concrete to attach them to. This exercise gives them the concrete thing first, and the predict-and-compare structure turns the result into a small surprise. Surprises are remembered, and an image a student built themselves is one they trust. For a teacher, that shared picture becomes a concrete starting point for the conceptual side of calculus, one the whole class has seen together and can be pointed back to when the formal ideas arrive.

**How it prepares students for later work.** When students later study how fast a function is changing at a single point, the second leading use of the Calculus Abacus, they will already sense that change can be tracked and shaped across an interval. When they meet the derivative, it will read as the change-curve made precise. When they meet limits and the old idea of an infinitely small step, those ideas will have somewhere to live, namely what happens to the change-curve as the steps between values shrink. None of this needs to be taught now. The exercise simply lays the visual groundwork so the later language has something to describe.
